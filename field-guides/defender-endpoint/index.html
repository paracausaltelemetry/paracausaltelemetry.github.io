Visual reference for Defender for Endpoint (MDE) on a host: the sensor and Defender Antivirus, how to read a detection name, attack surface reduction rules, the device timeline, response actions and live response, indicators, and the tampering to look for. Hosts, users and hashes below are placeholders. For Defender XDR incidents, Sentinel and the wider Advanced Hunting schema, see the Defender XDR and Sentinel field guide.

The mental model

Two engines share the host. Defender Antivirus blocks files and behaviour; the EDR sensor (MsSense.exe, built into Windows 10 and later) records what happens and streams it to the Defender portal. ASR rules, network protection and tamper protection harden the host around them.

MsMpEng.exe
Defender Antivirus's engine. Its service is WinDefend.
MsSense.exe
The EDR sensor. Its service is Sense.
Passive mode
Defender Antivirus runs but doesn't block, because another AV is primary. EDR still records.
EDR in block mode
Lets the EDR remediate things it sees even when Defender Antivirus is passive.
ASR rules
Attack surface reduction: blocks common attacker behaviour (Office spawning children, LSASS theft). Modes: Block, Audit, Warn, Off.
Tamper protection
Stops local changes to Defender settings, even by admins. Should be on everywhere.
Network protection
Blocks connections to bad domains and IPs, and enforces network indicators, for every process.
Controlled folder access
Only allowed apps can write to protected folders. Ransomware protection.
Device group
Groups devices for RBAC and automation level.
Automated investigation (AIR)
Investigates an alert and proposes or takes remediation, depending on the automation level.
Indicator
Your own hash, IP, URL, domain or certificate with an action: Allow, Audit, Warn, Block, or Block and remediate.

Reading a detection name

Read: the type tells you the most. Trojan, Backdoor, Ransom and HackTool deserve a look. Behavior: names come from behaviour monitoring rather than a file, so the alert's process tree matters more than the file. PUA: is potentially unwanted software, not malware.

On the host: Defender Antivirus

powershellGet-MpComputerStatus | Select-Object AMRunningMode, RealTimeProtectionEnabled, IsTamperProtected, AntivirusSignatureLastUpdated
Get-MpThreatDetection | Sort-Object InitialDetectionTime -Descending | Select-Object -First 10
Get-MpPreference | Select-Object ExclusionPath, ExclusionProcess, ExclusionExtension
& "$env:ProgramFiles\Windows Defender\MpCmdRun.exe" -Scan -ScanType 2
sc.exe query sense

ASR blocks and audits in Advanced Hunting

kqlDeviceEvents
| where Timestamp > ago(7d)
| where ActionType startswith "Asr"
| summarize count(), devices = dcount(DeviceId) by ActionType, FileName
| sort by count_ desc

Read: run new rules in Audit first and watch this query for a week. AsrLsassCredentialTheftAudited from your backup or monitoring agent is normal; from rundll32.exe it isn't.
